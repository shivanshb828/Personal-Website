Something that has taken me an embarrassingly long time to fully internalize: almost nothing that happens on the internet is a reliable signal about the world.

This sounds obvious. Everyone knows it. And yet I catch myself — and everyone I know — treating internet discourse as if it were a representative sample of human opinion. It isn't. Not even close.

## The selection problem

The people who write on the internet are not typical people. The people who argue on the internet are not typical people. The topics that go viral on the internet are not typical topics.

What you see on Twitter, Reddit, Substack — this is the output of a highly unusual population engaged in a particular kind of performance. It's real, in the sense that real people are doing it. But it doesn't map onto the distribution of beliefs, concerns, or experiences that actually exist in the world.

I think about this whenever I read takes about some cultural shift, some generational attitude, some new consensus. The signal-to-noise ratio is terrible. The confident takes are almost always too confident.

## The intensity problem

Online discourse rewards intensity. Strong takes get more engagement than nuanced ones. Conflict generates more attention than agreement. Certainty performs better than uncertainty.

So the internet selects for people and ideas that are unusually intense, unusually certain, unusually adversarial. And then we mistake that selection pressure for the underlying distribution of thought.

## What this means practically

I've started treating internet discourse the way I treat self-reported data in surveys: as evidence of something, just not necessarily what it claims to be evidence of.

Someone expressing an opinion online is evidence that this person holds this view with enough intensity to express it publicly. That's all. It's not evidence of how common the view is. It's not evidence that the view is reasonable. It's not evidence that the events being reacted to are as significant as the reaction suggests.

The world is larger, stranger, and quieter than the internet implies. Getting outside — literally, but also in terms of information diet — is a kind of calibration.
